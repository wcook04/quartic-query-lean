### Rubric-exposed teach-back

We compare classical and quantum access to one total Boolean input, a board of columns. A winner has an all-one string and a correct certificate hidden at an address formed from hard partial-function answers. Its pointers certify that all other columns contain zeros. Quantum comparisons can decode addresses cheaply; they need no ordered ranking among losers. A weighted tournament process collects a short list containing the winner with high probability, using amplification to sample without explicitly estimating weights; then it checks the list's full certificates. A classical run must either find one erased zero or reach a planted true cell. Enough address learning can happen in only a few columns, while a light cell guess already predicts many independent hard answers, so its joint light-and-correct probability is small. This produces Q bounded by roughly sqrt(m) and R bounded below by roughly m^(2−1/k). Choose a proposed exponent below four, fix a sufficiently large k, then let m grow so the polynomial margin beats all logarithms. The separate universal quartic bound gives the upper endpoint.

## Lecturer teach-back: post-reading, scaffold-exposed

This reconstruction was made after completing the article and its answers. It records what the lecturer review takes the argument to mean. It does not count as a fresh unaided attempt or establish that the article taught these facts to a person.

### Why the quantum search can tolerate cycles

Fix an input board. The single experiment for each unordered pair defines probabilities a_ij with a_ij+a_ji=1 and a_ii=0. If a genuine winner exists, its all-one string prevents every rival pointer from disqualifying it, regardless of the rival's decoded label. Correct decoding of the winner's own address disqualifies its rival. Thus it beats each rival with probability at least 1−epsilon. Losers need not be ordered. [Lead: lines 191–205.]

The ideal process keeps weights equal to products of probabilities of beating previously selected pivots. A pivot is chosen proportionally to its weight and permanently appended to the list. Pairing the ordered terms (i,p) and (p,i) uses complementary probabilities to show that expected total weight falls by at least half each stage. The winner's weight remains above one half until selected. Consequently, a missing winner forces a nontrivially large total weight, and Markov's inequality converts geometric decay of the expected total into a small missing-winner probability. This requires expectation over histories, not deterministic halving on every history. [Lead: lines 207–226.]

The algorithm does not evaluate those weights. It chooses a candidate uniformly in superposition and runs fresh controlled comparisons against the classical pivot list. Conditional on the input, history and candidate, the work registers are separate, so the joint mass of success and label i is w_i/m. Conditioning on success gives w_i divided by the total weight. Amplitude amplification changes the total coefficient of the normalized good state, while preserving the relative label probabilities inside it. All retained workspace is part of that good state and of the preparation/inverse. The capped unknown-success routine supplies the finite query guarantee; its trial-count construction is accepted on this route. [Lead: lines 229–266.]

Before the winner is listed, its retained weight gives a lower success threshold, so premature sampling failure has bounded probability. After it is listed, later failure is harmless because the list is retained. Final local verification is essential: a comparison dominator can be a nonwinner. Every nonwinner fails a test at every address, whereas the winner at its correct address has no violations. The algorithm pays for cheap-comparison search plus verification of a short list, rather than nesting full verification inside search over every column. [Lead: lines 268–294.]

### Why a classical algorithm cannot cheaply notice the plant

Minimax supplies one hard distribution for the partial input. Independent draws provide all address blocks; each dummy string has an independent uniform zero, and cells are blank. An independently chosen target column is planted by erasing its zero and filling only its true cell. Fixing a deterministic tree's coins makes the two runs identical until a changed bit is read. The output gap is therefore at most the probability of finding the erased zero plus the probability of visiting the planted cell, both evaluated on the dummy run. [Lead: lines 298–311.]

For the zero term, let Z count columns whose zero is found. Independence of the target gives Pr(hit)=EZ/m after conditioning on the entire dummy run. Early discoveries have conditional hazard at most 2/m; late ones require enough distinct reads to be charged to the global budget. Together they bound EZ. A uniform marginal for the target would not be enough if its selection depended on the run. [Lead: lines 313–319.]

For the cell term, a heavy target column is costly enough to count directly. For one fixed column and global time, consider the joint event that the complete run is light and that the query names its true cell. A simulator receives independent outside data, forwards at most L target queries, stops before query L+1, and always outputs an address. At the specified cell proposal it extracts the proposed address from the query position without reading the cell. On the target event it does not abort and is correct. On other events its default may also be correct, which only enlarges the success event used as an upper bound. [Lead: lines 323–350.]

The simulator's total allowance L implies the separately capped direct-product allowance L for each instance. Independent outside information is permitted. Hence its unconditional success, including all abort branches, is at most (2/3)^r. The proof bounds the joint light-and-correct event; it does not condition the input distribution on staying light. Summing over times and averaging over the independently selected column gives the light-cell term without an extra factor m. Together with heavy columns and zero hits, the output gap is strictly below one third at the proposed budget. Every bounded-error algorithm would require a gap of at least one third. The common bound for every fixed-coin tree also bounds their mixture. [Lead: lines 346–379.]

### Why the conclusion is four

For any proposed alpha below four, first choose one fixed k with 2−1/k−alpha/2 positive. Then let m grow. The lower estimate on R divided by the upper estimate on (1+Q)^alpha diverges because a fixed positive power defeats the logarithmic loss. The dependence of constants and starting thresholds on k is harmless in this order and prevents an unlicensed substitution k=k(m). The family excludes every smaller exponent. The imported universal fourth-power upper bound makes four admissible, giving equality. [Lead: lines 384–398.]
