## Mathematician's continuous reading log

### Reader, scope and standard of judgment

This is an emulated reader: a research mathematician working in analysis and combinatorics, comfortable with finite-dimensional linear algebra, probability, asymptotics and rigorous quantifiers, but with no previous course in query complexity or quantum computing. These are constructed reading reactions, not observations about a real participant. I read every paragraph, displayed calculation, question, answer and caption in the current Markdown exposition. The relevant criterion is not whether an unfamiliar word is eventually defined somewhere in the packet. It is whether I can say, at each point, what problem the current object solves and why the next object is needed. In this sense the exposition is much improved but remains a successful second encounter for a quantum-algorithms student more than a first encounter for the assigned mathematician.

### Introduction: an attractive claim, then several simultaneous debts

The first sentence gives me a plausible model: the function is fixed, the input is hidden, and information arrives through charged accesses. Random coins and adaptive choices are familiar. The oracle display is the first genuine loss of footing. I can parse an indexed vector after being told that the kets are basis vectors, but the prose has not said that, and it has not explained why an xor oracle is the relevant access operation. I know that a vector can be a superposition; I do not yet know how a quantum computation turns that vector into a probabilistic output or how a query count differs from a count of revealed bits. The detailed worst-case convention is useful and should stay. It becomes much easier to remember after one ordinary Boolean example has given R and Q concrete meanings.

The next paragraph distinguishes total and partial functions correctly. As a mathematician, I understand a function on a restricted domain immediately. I do not understand yet why this restriction is the turning point in the subject. A promise appears to remove inconvenient inputs, but I need one reason that doing so can change the classical/quantum comparison radically. The exponent infimum then asks me to manipulate a formal statement involving measures whose behavior I have not seen. I can follow the quantifiers and I appreciate the addition of 1 inside the power, but that is syntactic understanding rather than a reason to care about four.

The theorem and universal upper bound establish the result's importance. The citation and authorship distinctions are useful: the construction theorem, the earlier quartic upper bound, the cubic conjecture and the paper's explicit constants are not conflated. I do not need all the explicit constants this early, but the fixed-k family is a good statement to return to at the end.

The exponent arithmetic is an excellent orientation device once I permit myself to treat L as an accepted cost. It says that one factor comes from the number of columns and another from the cost hidden inside a column. Two pieces of shorthand worry me. First, a certificate is said to sit in a cell, but later verification accesses bits outside the cell. I initially picture a self-contained proof, whereas the stored object is witness data whose validity depends on other input fields. Second, the classical picture is a distributional lower bound that charges a constant fraction of columns, not an algorithmic requirement to fully decode every address. The later proof fixes the latter misconception well; it would help if the introductory metaphor did not invite it.

The paragraph on the parameter order is clear and worth retaining. It reassures me that a familiar power-versus-logarithm argument will eventually finish the theorem. The historical paragraph supplies credit and a reason this construction matters. The next paragraph, however, teaches an entire earlier composition before I know the new construction: AND–OR, certificates, composition and three cost formulas appear together. I can check the arithmetic but do not yet see why these are the relevant products. I would retain the cubic limit as motivation and read the detailed comparison more profitably after the board or after the present two bounds.

The prerequisite paragraph is candid but describes a different reader from this panel. It assumes the query model, Grover search, amplification, uncomputation and minimax. Naming these assumptions repaired a hidden-prerequisite problem in the previous round; it did not teach them to the present reader. The route paragraph and optional proof designation are useful. I would choose the lower-bound route first because it matches my background, then return to the quantum section with a short preparation on the measurement rule and amplification contract.

### Section 1: the first complete mathematical foothold

The bit-to-sign convention, 4-by-4 Walsh matrix and one-sided thresholds are all easy to inspect. I welcome the explicit negative example. Many mathematical constructions are symmetric in sign, so explaining that a large negative value is outside the promise prevents an incorrect symmetry assumption. The three eight-bit inputs are small enough to compute, and their shared second block reduces the amount of arithmetic I must hold in mind.

The geometric paragraph is the best entry point for my reader. A normalized inner product between a sign vector and a transformed sign vector is familiar, and the three cases—alignment, orthogonality, reversed alignment—make the labels memorable. This is more than a check of numbers: it provides an interpretation of what Forrelation detects. I would keep referring to this geometry when the k-block product appears.

The two-query experiment is where the exposition crosses from familiar mathematics into unfamiliar computational physics. The scalar formula is verifiable, but the probability Phi squared requires the two-outcome measurement rule. A short sentence explaining projection probability as a squared norm would let me use the linear algebra I already know. “Apply the signs with one query” also needs an accepted phase-oracle fact or an explanation of phase kickback later; it is not a consequence of ordinary matrix multiplication alone. The contrast between the chosen probability-one example and a promised one-input with probability one quarter is useful because it prevents me from believing that every instance is exactly decided by one trial.

The final two paragraphs are particularly effective. Acceptance of the negative input is harmless for the partial problem and dangerous inside a total function. The input counts and the empty small-size lower bound keep the example in its proper role. The section ends with a concrete question—how to reject an unpromised input despite the cheap decoder—that Section 2 actually answers.

### Section 2: a good board reached through too much notation

The opening states the problem correctly: the total function cannot simply forget unpromised inputs. I then meet the general column before the four-column board. The field list is precise, but it requires me to retain m, r, k, ell, an array of size 2 to the r, transcripts, addresses and pointers at once. The figure caption helps organize these components. For a first encounter I would prefer to see the small board establish their roles, then read the general dimensions as a transcription of that picture.

The winning condition is understandable and the uniqueness proof is short and satisfying. An all-one column cannot simultaneously contain the zero another winner would have to point to. This local incompatibility is more memorable than a separate uniqueness lemma would be. The attribution paragraph is also unusually informative: it identifies the addressed storage from cheat sheets, direct zero pointers from pointer functions, and the particular combination used here. The explanation of r as a union-bound resource is welcome; twenty no longer looks like an unexplained magic constant. I cannot yet prove the per-guess bound, but I know what job its exponent will do.

The four-column specialization clearly declares that r has been reduced and that no hardness claim survives. This is exactly the right warning. I can locate the winner, understand why column 2 has no true address, and see how the displayed pointers certify the other columns' zeros. The board's raw inputs differ from those in the linked long-record worked board and checker. That does not invalidate either board, but it means the record is not currently a literal expansion of this example. The changed inputs have an important teaching purpose here: they make the decoder deterministic on the carried examples. The transition should say so rather than silently inviting byte-for-byte comparison.

The transcript paragraph is strong. A fixed public circuit and proposed gate values are distinguished, and induction gives a unique consistent transcript on every input, promised or not. The changed-board paragraph then makes the domain bit necessary in one step. The gate equations and address-value tests can all pass while the domain-output test fails. This is the first place where I can explain in my own words what totalization costs and why the quantum decoder alone cannot define the winning relation.

The local-test list is useful because it converts a large certificate check into individually cheap checks. The concrete pointer test reads two address bits and then one target bit, so adaptive access is visible. I can compute 371 from the list and understand why output tests are additional to consistency equations even when output gates are among the S gates. Question 1 is well chosen: it tests the exact distinction the section just taught, and the answer resolves all four addresses without introducing another gadget.

### Section 3: a clean contract, with one opaque input

The general Walsh product is a natural extension of Section 1, and the operator-norm bound is transparent. The cheap decoder gives a concrete statistical separation between probabilities at least c and at most c/4. Chernoff bounds are familiar, so once the experiment and oracle cost are accepted the repetition count is understandable. I still need phase kickback as a bridge from the bit oracle to a sign diagonal, but that need can be localized.

The exact-circuit paragraph is dense but purposeful. The integer numerator explains why domain membership can be computed exactly; the fast transform explains why a giant path sum need not be expanded. I do not need to audit ripple-carry arithmetic on this route. The classical-hardness paragraph is the one major ingredient with no mechanism. Accepting a theorem is appropriate, but one qualitative sentence connecting it to the earlier Fourier geometry would help me understand what local classical observations fail to detect. The full Gaussian proof belongs in the record.

The final input-length formula is useful evidence that the construction is a finite Boolean function of polynomially many coordinates in m. Its scale is startling: m is not the input length, and the quantum cost is not square root of the total number of bits. I need the query-model distinction stated here, especially because a normal running-time algorithm would have a different relationship to an enormous explicitly written input. The same-circuit sentence closes a logical loop and should remain.

### Section 4: an accessible probability argument inside an unfamiliar quantum implementation

The failed nested-search cost is the right motivation. It tells me why comparing columns cheaply is not merely an implementation preference. The comparison rule is concrete, including the tie breaker and self-comparison convention. Keeping all decoder labels in workspace is harder: I understand orthogonal branches as linear algebra, but reversible implementation and coherent access need a preparation bridge for the assigned background. The reliability argument against the winner is excellent; it depends on the winning column's all-one string and its own correct address, not on the rival being promised.

The dominating nonwinner and cycle among losers are both necessary examples. The former explains final verification; the latter explains why ordinary sorting is not the algorithm. They are small modifications of the existing board, so I can verify the failure modes without learning new input geometry. The paragraph on earlier search methods is useful only if the relevant hypotheses are stated, as they now are. I can understand that arbitrary loser biases need not implement a fixed predicate with bounded error even without knowing the cited algorithms.

The ideal weighted process is comfortable mathematics. The table teaches what happens for each pivot. The paired-sum identity is short, exact and robust to cycles. The lower bound on the winner's surviving weight plus Markov's inequality gives a satisfying argument that a short list contains the winner. I can retell this part independently. The toy, however, uses only weights zero and one. One fractional comparison example would help separate the actual proof from ordinary survivor elimination; the record has such a variant, but the relationship should be introduced before its fractions.

The sampling paragraph contains the central quantum insight. Fresh registers multiply masses at fixed label; orthogonal labels add the masses; conditioning on success produces the ideal law. For my background, those are the two crucial linear-algebra facts. They deserve more emphasis than the later amplification constants. I can accept an amplification contract once it promises bounded cost, a controlled failure probability above a threshold, and preservation of the conditional label distribution.

The full trial recipe is where I would slow down most or skip. It introduces angle parameterization, repeated Grover rotations, a mixture of trial lengths, a split at three quarters and several constants. Each line is meaningful, but together they teach a second algorithm before finishing the first. The exact conditional law and worst-case cap matter to the construction; the particular 19/40 calculation does not determine its exponent. This remains the weighting issue identified in the previous round's plan to move the recipe out.

The stage description repairs an important potential misunderstanding: a successful label is measured and becomes a classical pivot; failures may depend on history; stopping after the winner is listed is safe. The exact stage on the cycled board is valuable, especially the probability-one-half case where Grover iterations do not improve success. Question 2 then gives a contrasting one-quarter case where one iteration succeeds certainly. These two calculations make the angle formula less abstract.

Verification reconnects the algorithm to the domain-bit example. A nonwinner fails some test at every address, including an off-promise decoder label. The separate zero-input and one-input error arguments are clear. The final cost count is long but can be read at two levels: the leading square-root factor is the theorem's point, while the logarithmic factors certify a finite overhead. I would retain the three main cost categories and link the loose products; I would not spend equal teaching time on the number six and on the exact sampling identity.

### Section 5: the strongest route for this reader

The minimax distribution is a familiar convexity idea once its purpose is stated: one common law must defeat every capped deterministic strategy so that independent copies can be used. The dummy/planted pair is clear, and the figure caption correctly says that the four-column drawing illustrates locations rather than a small hard distribution. The two first-difference mechanisms organize the entire lower bound. Counting a true-cell visit even when its bit remains unchanged is explicitly recognized as an overcount, which prevents an unnecessary objection.

The erased-zero argument has one wording defect that a probabilist notices immediately: the next-query calculation must condition on a transcript prefix, not on the whole completed transcript. The long record states the correct prefix argument. Once that is repaired, the early/late split is intuitive and rigorous. The dependent-column example is excellent: the chosen index can have a uniform marginal while still be correlated with discoveries. Question 3 tests a real distinction rather than arithmetic for its own sake.

The true-cell paragraph correctly allows guessing and multiple attempts. This is essential. An address-shaped query itself constitutes a proposed answer tuple; the proof does not require a prior semantic act of learning the address. The heavy/light split then makes sense as a budget decomposition.

The separate-cap direct product is well taught. The pooled-budget counterexample and the xor side-information example each remove one tempting but false weakening of its assumptions. In the optional proof, posterior factorization along a deterministic decision-tree history is recognizable, and the conditional value functions form the right supermartingale product. The numerical AND example shows that a value can rise on one branch even though its conditional expectation cannot. I would keep this proof for an analysis/combinatorics reader; it is an appropriate use of our background, not excessive specialization.

The success-given-lightness counterexample is also worth its space. It makes the later simulator reduction precise. I can now distinguish a bound on a joint event from a claim about a conditional distribution selected by the algorithm. The simulator paragraph is still the hardest classical step because it is a reduction between two computational tasks. The three-budget paragraph is a genuine improvement: global G, a shared cap L in one column, and a per-instance cap L have different roles. A total cap implies the separate caps; the direct product permits more resources than the simulator uses. The trace link should land directly on the trace rather than on another general proof paragraph.

The final union bound is clear once fixed column and fixed query time remain visible. Summing over query times gives G, and averaging over the independent planted column removes the sum over columns. The discrepancy contradiction is standard, and the same-circuit observation is a useful closure. I can finish this route without pretending to understand the quantum implementation.

### Section 6: the payoff is familiar, with one misleading shorthand

The floor estimates are now given with a sufficient threshold, and alpha is explicitly restricted to be nonnegative before monotonicity is used. I checked these as repairs rather than treating them as still missing. The power gap is the familiar fact that a positive power eventually defeats a fixed logarithmic power. The parameter order is exact and the counterexample t at least 2 to the k makes nonuniformity tangible.

The phrase that k equals two gives “exactly the cube” compresses too much. What is exact is the threshold power 4 minus 2/k; the displayed ratio argument proves divergence only below that power, and logarithmic losses remain. Question 4 asks for alpha_tot at least 3.5. For that literal target, k equals four suffices because it excludes every strictly smaller exponent. Its answer chooses k equals five, which excludes the endpoint power 3.5 itself. The intended stronger target should be named if that answer is retained. The matching universal upper bound can reasonably be accepted here. A reader who wants its mechanism can use the record's short inequality chain before attempting the full sensitivity argument.

### Section 7, answers and closing matter

The Lean section is a sensible endpoint if read as a description of available statements, not as a new prerequisite. The exact statement versus explicit-constant variants are separated. I have not run Lean or Comparator and do not treat their reported success as something this review independently verified. The suggestion to inspect the second winning clause on the altered board is the right invitation: it gives me a mathematical question I can ask of a formal definition. It is much more actionable than a general instruction to inspect a large formalization.

I read all four answers. The first three correspond to their questions and use the same board or probability setup. Answer 4 answers the stronger endpoint-exclusion question described above, not the literal infimum-lower-bound question; the wording should be aligned. The boundary qualification should also be carried back to the sentence in Section 6. The AI-use statement identifies the origin of the theorem and construction; this is clear enough for the reading task. The bibliography placeholder and reference definitions are source apparatus rather than additional mathematical exposition. I did not infer that a rendered bibliography or every external link had been tested from reading the Markdown.

### What this reader would now retell, and the retelling the exposition should secure

My first unaided retelling would probably be: put a hard address in each column, let the quantum algorithm read addresses cheaply and find the winner using roughly square-root search, then force a classical algorithm to solve the address problem in many columns. That is a useful outline but it contains two risks. It may overstate “solve” when guessing is allowed, and it may hide the tournament behind the misleading phrase ordinary search.

The more accurate retelling that the exposition should make stable is this: the input contains independently assignable fields, and the winning relation makes a stored transcript-and-pointer witness verifiable even on unpromised address inputs. A comparison need be reliable only against an actual winner, while complementarity holds for every pair. The ideal weighted process halves total mass in expectation and keeps enough winner mass; coherent preparation and amplification reproduce its conditional pivot law. Verification rejects a dominating nonwinner. On the classical side, couple a dummy input to one with a winner planted in an independent uniform column. Before the first changed bit, the runs coincide. An erased zero is unlikely to be found under the total budget. A fixed query into a light column's true cell gives a separately capped predictor of all address bits, so its joint light-and-hit event is exponentially unlikely. Union over query times, add the heavy columns, and compare the two costs. Finally fix a target exponent, then one k, then let m grow.

I would finish the classical route and the exponent deduction. I would finish the quantum route only by accepting a small number of clearly named standard facts or doing the missing preparation first. The revisions with the largest benefit are therefore not more theorem detail: they are a short finite-dimensional quantum bridge, clearer separation of the amplification contract from its numerical implementation, and better continuity when moving from the carried board to record examples.

## Undergraduate reading log

### Perspective and scope

This is an emulated final-year mathematics/CS undergraduate perspective, not a report of a real person’s reactions. The reader knows probability, linear algebra, asymptotic algorithm analysis and the basic P/NP distinction, but has not taken quantum computing or query complexity. I read every paragraph of the current `exposition.md`, the companion’s abstract and introduction through §1.3, and the record’s reading guide, §1, §2.2, and the linked passages for the toy local tests, logarithmic quantum cost, minimax, erased-zero search, capped simulator and universal upper endpoint. ### Introduction: the question is interesting before its notation is intelligible

The opening clause about reading bits is a good start. I can imagine an algorithm asking for an input coordinate and using earlier replies. However, the first quantum-oracle formula is already outside my background. I recognize a linear mapping once the basis and tensor factors are specified, but ket notation, a workspace register and an XOR oracle have not been introduced. The supplied Buhrman–de Wolf survey actually supplies a small vector-and-measurement bridge before its quantum oracle. Citing its section as a prerequisite later does not make the first paragraph self-contained for this reader.

The phrase “on every input” is mathematically important. I understand the words, but I need the random experiment made explicit: fix the input first, then vary the algorithm’s coins or measurement outcomes. I would otherwise be tempted to import the average-case viewpoint used in familiar algorithm analyses. I would have benefited from one four-bit OR tree here: query until a one is found, see why the all-zero input can force more reads, then state the classical/quantum scaling at larger size. That would give a baseline for what a separation and a query lower bound mean before introducing the extremal family.

The partial-versus-total distinction is explained more clearly. The promise describes the allowed inputs, and a total function must return the right answer everywhere. Nevertheless, the first appearance of `O_k(1)` asks me to track two asymptotic parameters before their roles are settled. The theorem’s `C_k` later hints at the right convention, but I would like the dependence stated at the earlier use. The infimum definition is readable as mathematics; what is missing is the plain universal question it formalizes. One constant must work for all input lengths and all total Boolean functions. This also explains why a two-parameter counterexample family can refute a single candidate power.

The theorem display contains enough precision to verify the final exponent, but on first contact the coefficient 1/400 and the sixth power of a logarithm feel as salient as the central powers of m. I would remember them without knowing their role. The account immediately explains that these constants come from later counts, which is honest, but that suggests a better order: make the mechanism memorable first and restore quantitative precision once the reader knows the objects being counted.

The one-line exponent calculation is highly useful. I can see that a classical contribution of m times an almost-linear factor is compared with a quantum square root. I do need a warning that the `≈` signs are bookkeeping for one lower bound and one upper bound, not matching estimates for both complexities. I also need the noun certificate unpacked. An NP witness is something I already know, but a proposed transcript stored in the input is a different object from an external prover’s message. At this point I do not know which one is intended.

The long mechanism paragraph names the two places where planting changes the input, which is an improvement over a vague assertion that the algorithm must learn an address. Its wording “Learning them costs about L” still suggests that all r answers can be learned for the cost of one instance. The eventual proof only needs the one-sided statement that below L total queries the chance of getting the entire address right is small. That distinction matters pedagogically because otherwise I invent a classical shortcut to explain the missing r factor.

The warning about choosing k before letting m grow is well placed in principle. I cannot yet appreciate all of it, but it gives me something to revisit. The historical paragraph then becomes a wall of proper names and exponents attached to construction tools I have not seen. I would keep the credits but move some of the chronology until pointers and cheat sheets have a concrete meaning. The next paragraph’s old cubic arithmetic is much more helpful than the list. Its crucial claim is the small certificate for AND-OR, yet that claim could be made almost immediate by showing the two certificate shapes on a two-by-two array. The reason the old route reaches only the cube is a teaching goal, so this deserves a genuine explanatory step.

The declared audience is the main mismatch revealed by the opening. The text says it is for a graduate student who knows the query models, Grover, amplitude amplification, uncomputation, Yao and conditional decision-tree probabilities. That is an honest account of the current draft, and the prior revision did improve its honesty. It does not match the present assignment’s undergraduate newcomer. The remedy is a short, selective bridge into the exact operations used, rather than pretending the prerequisites are merely linear algebra.

The learning outcomes and the two course routes are strong. I now know I am supposed to build a board, run a tournament and explain the classical address cost. The roadmap promises a sustained example, with a deliberately optional direct-product proof. The links to the longer account and the specialist companion make sense. I would keep all of these navigational choices while reducing the opening’s total inventory of unfamiliar terms. I also need a sentence that m is the number of columns rather than the total input length n introduced in the first line.

### Section 1: the first successful bridge, followed by a quantum step taken too quickly

The eight-bit example is the first place where the material is fully within my reach. The bit-to-sign convention is explicit; the four-by-four matrix and its coordinate order let me perform the products. The positive, zero and negative cases make the one-sided promise visible. I understand why the negative input is not a zero-input. This is much better than defining the thresholds without examples.

The geometric paragraph is especially effective. The normalized Walsh transform preserves length; matching, opposite and orthogonal vectors produce the three values. The next paragraph crosses from a matrix element to a two-query experiment. I need the rule that the probability of a projection outcome is the squared modulus of the inner product. Without that, the formula for Φ is familiar mathematics but its interpretation as a query algorithm is asserted rather than taught. The later phase-kickback reference will require another short bridge: I have not yet seen why an ordinary bit-query oracle can apply the input-dependent signs in one call.

The added input with Φ=1/2 is valuable because it stops the three extremal inputs from teaching that one projection solves the promise. The first general column description introduces a large collection of parameters and indexed fields. This is logically efficient, but after the concrete Forrelation input it feels like a step away from the examples-first route. A two-address-input column with four cells could introduce the same objects, followed by the general sizes. The existing general picture would then summarize something already understood.

The declaration that every bit is input and every assignment is allowed is essential. I now understand that transcripts can be false and that a separate promise about their correctness has not been smuggled into the outer function. The address depends on promised values; when an address input is unpromised, the column has no true address. The winning definition makes that condition explicit. The uniqueness argument is short and convincing: two all-one winning strings cannot each supply a zero for the other’s pointer.

The addressing paragraph explains why r grows logarithmically and why the number of cells stays polynomial. It also gives credit in terms of actual mechanisms. That is more useful than the earlier chronology because I can now attach the predecessor ideas to objects in front of me. I would underline the distinction between a cell address and a row pointer. Both are two bits in the toy, but their equal lengths are accidental. The rule that the pointer into column 1 is encoded by the cell’s own label is a chosen assignment for the example, not a structural identity of the construction.

The four-column board is a good size. I can check its strings, locate the true cells and follow every displayed pointer. The warning that r has been reduced and the tiny board has no asymptotic hardness is clear. That issue was fixed previously and should not be raised again as though absent. The problem is a narrower one: the text announces that column 1 wins before explaining its transcript condition. At that point I can check the arrows but must trust the unnamed 180-gate object.

The transcript paragraph gives the right logical induction: all gate equations force the unique true evaluation. The public-circuit versus proposed-gate-values distinction is also clear. “Shannon expansion” feels like unnecessary specialist terminology in this account; the exact-circuit existence and its output bits are the useful facts. The local-test paragraph now traces a pointer lookup explicitly, which is a real improvement. In the PDF, Figure 1 distinguishes strings, address inputs, cells and pointers visibly. Its arrows nevertheless have two meanings: mathematical selection of a cell and stored pointers into strings. Figure 2 is legible and keeps the pointer structure small, but its empty labeled cell boxes necessarily hide the transcript checks. The text should be clear about what the picture alone verifies.

### Section 3: a good interface with an overly compressed circuit explanation

The three-fact organization is exactly right. A decoder, an exact circuit and a classical lower bound do different jobs. I can accept the deep hardness theorem as a stated input while following how the construction uses it. The general Walsh definition needs only a minor notation gloss: a·b supplies the parity of the binary coordinate products. That would let me reconstruct the larger matrix from the small one.

The decoder paragraph exposes a more substantial prerequisite gap. “Phase kickback” is the reason the supplied bit oracle can implement one sign diagonal in one query, but the name alone does not explain the operation to this reader. The supplied de Wolf notes give a compact calculation with the answer state proportional to |0⟩−|1⟩; that is the right scale of bridge. The repetition argument itself is recognizable probability once the quantum experiment is understood. I would explicitly connect c=2^(−10k) to the hidden constant in O_k: it makes the later fixed-k discipline concrete.

The exact-circuit paragraph is too dense for its teaching role. It states the integer numerator, two threshold comparisons, an output encoding, a fast Walsh transform, ripple-carry arithmetic and a numerical circuit-size coefficient in a single block. The key point for me is that the exact certification circuit is near-linear in m up to logarithms, so its transcript can be checked cheaply enough. The fast transform is the reason dense m-by-m multiplication does not ruin that size. One sentence about that structure would be more useful than another coefficient.

The lower-bound statement and its attribution are appropriately concise. The total input-size formula is welcome but needs an interpretive sentence. Since the array is large, I need to be reminded that the input is supplied for coordinate access and that allocating a data structure or physically storing it is not a cost being compared by R and Q. The result is a relation between two query measures, not a claim that m equals the number of input bits or that all other computational resources vanish in practice.

### Section 4: the new idea is well motivated, but the worked stage should arrive sooner

The opening failed algorithm is excellent. A square-root search over columns multiplied by a square-root check per column is too expensive. This immediately explains why the new search must use cheap comparisons and postpone expensive verification. I would keep this as the controlling motivation for every later detail.

The comparison rule can be implemented from the board, but a short four-case table for the two disqualification flags would reduce initial reconstruction. The two obstruction examples do valuable work. A dominating nonwinner explains why the final check is indispensable. The loser cycle explains why an ordinary sorted order is unavailable. ### What this reader could say afterwards, and what the account should make possible

**Likely present takeaway.** “There is a board with a special column, hidden by hard Forrelation addresses. A quantum tournament finds it in roughly the square root of the number of columns, while classical algorithms need roughly the number of columns times the cost of an address. That gives an exponent approaching four.” This is a correct slogan, but it can conceal several missing distinctions: m is not total input length, an address is not free, a comparison winner need not be a genuine winner, and the classical proof is about event probabilities rather than a deterministic requirement to learn an address.

**Desired teach-back.** “The outer function is total because every candidate’s promise, value, transcript, string and pointers can be checked exactly. A real winner defeats every rival cheaply even when the rival’s address inputs are unpromised. Complementary comparison probabilities shrink expected surviving weight, and a retained short list is checked at the end. Classically, a dummy input and a planted one differ only at an erased zero and a single correctly addressed cell. A small global query budget rarely finds the erased zero, leaves almost all columns light, and gives an exponentially small chance per query of naming the full true address in a light column. These are lower and upper bounds for the same function. A fixed k with a strict exponent gap defeats each proposed power below four.”

The second account is reachable from the draft’s best examples. The next revision should concentrate on the bridges and ordering that let this reader actually reconstruct it.

### What I could tell a colleague now, and what the intended understanding should be

**Likely current retelling.** “There is a large artificial table with a certificate hidden at a hard address. **What the exposition should enable.** I should be able to explain that comparisons need only be reliable when one candidate is the genuine winner; there may be no correct ordering among losers. Fresh coherent comparisons assign each label a success mass proportional to a product of winning probabilities. Capped amplitude amplification preserves the relative successful-label probabilities, so it samples the ideal weight process without estimating the weights. That process quickly lists the winner when one exists, and exact local verification makes the algorithm correct on every raw input. On the classical side, a query to the planted cell predicts the whole hidden address even if it was guessed, so the simulator and direct product bound cover arbitrary adaptive strategies. The result concerns oracle calls and an asymptotic family, with the quantifier order controlling the approach to the fourth power.

That understanding is within reach of the existing material.
