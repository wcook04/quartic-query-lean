## 3. Why the planted-input argument works

The proof compares two distributions on inputs to the same total function.

In the **dummy input**, every \(X\)-column has one independently uniform zero, the partial-function inputs are drawn independently from a hard distribution, and all certificate cells are blank.

In the **planted input**, an independent uniform column \(I\) has its zero erased. Its true certificate cell is filled with valid circuit transcripts and pointers to the zeros in the other columns.

The dummy input has function value zero. The planted input has function value one. The inputs differ only at the erased zero and within that one certificate cell.

If a deterministic query tree produces different outputs on the two inputs, its dummy run must encounter a changed coordinate. This yields the two contributions:

| Possible encounter | Bound |
|---|---:|
| Erased zero | \(4G/m^2\), or \(2G/m^2\) by the alternative search proof |
| True certificate cell | \(G/(mL)+G(2/3)^r\) |

The proof must justify these contributions under the adaptive query process. The rewritten section makes those justifications explicit.

### Discovering a zero

For the paper’s early/late argument, fix a positive-probability transcript prefix. Before a column’s zero is discovered, the transcript only excludes the distinct rows already queried in that column. Its zero remains uniform among the unqueried rows.

An early discovery occurs while fewer than \(m/2\) distinct positions have previously been queried in its column. Its conditional probability is at most \(2/m\) per query, giving
\[
\mathbb E Z_{\mathrm{early}}\le\frac{2G}{m}.
\]

Every late-discovered column has consumed at least \(m/2\) queries. Since the entire run uses at most \(G\) queries,
\[
Z_{\mathrm{late}}\le\frac{2G}{m}
\]
on every execution. Hence
\[
\mathbb EZ\le\frac{4G}{m}.
\]

Repeated queries and branches that stop early are handled explicitly.

The formal source uses a different finite-world induction, giving the stronger endpoint
\[
\mathbb EZ\le\frac{2G}{m},
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{erase}}\le\frac{2G}{m^2}.
\]
I retained and explained both routes. The stronger endpoint supports the numerical bound, but does not certify that the early/late intermediate statements were formalized.

The baseline’s further refinement
\[
p_{\mathrm{erase}}\le\frac{2G}{m(m+1)}
\]
is also retained with its ordinary proof. Its scan example establishes sharpness for an **expectation-cost inequality**; it does not determine the optimal distinguishing success at every prescribed worst-case budget.

### Querying the true certificate cell

A column is heavy if the complete dummy run makes more than \(L\) queries in total to its partial-function inputs. Counting queries and averaging over the independent uniform \(I\) gives
\[
\Pr[I\text{ is heavy}]\le\frac{G}{mL}.
\]

For a fixed column and proposed query time, the simulator:

- receives data jointly independent of the entire target tuple;
- answers outside queries from that data;
- charges queries to the target inputs against a shared counter;
- aborts **before** target query \(L+1\);
- outputs a proposed cell’s address before reading that cell’s contents.

The cap holds on every simulator branch. It therefore satisfies the separate input caps required by the direct-product prerequisite, giving success at most \((2/3)^r\).

The critical event containment is
\[
\{\text{column nonheavy and proposed query hits its true cell}\}
\subseteq
\{\text{capped simulator guesses the true address}\}.
\]

Thus the proof bounds a **joint event under the original distribution**. It does not condition the hard input distribution on nonheaviness.

Union bounding over the \(G\) query times and then averaging over \(I\) yields
\[
\Pr[\text{true-cell visit}]
\le
\frac{G}{mL}+G(2/3)^r.
\]
There is no additional factor \(m\): the sum over columns is divided by \(m\) when the planted column is averaged.

## 4. Conditioning on nonheaviness is a real potential error

I included a small counterexample showing why the preceding distinction is necessary.

Let \(P\) be AND on the promised inputs
\[
\{01,10,11\},
\]
with the uniform distribution. Every one-query algorithm succeeds with probability at most \(2/3\).

Now consider an algorithm that queries the first bit, stops with answer zero if that bit is zero, and otherwise queries the second bit. With \(L=1\), its original run is nonheavy exactly on input \(01\). Therefore
\[
\Pr[\text{correct}\mid\text{nonheavy}]=1.
\]

A capped simulator that aborts before the second query and defaults to zero has
\[
\Pr[\text{capped simulator correct}]=\frac23,
\qquad
\Pr[\text{correct and nonheavy}]=\frac13.
\]

The unconditional bound remains valid. Applying the same \(2/3\) bound after selecting the nonheavy executions would be false. This example isolates the probability error without changing the theorem’s assumptions.
