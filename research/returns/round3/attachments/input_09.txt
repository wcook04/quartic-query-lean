## 1. The theorem survives the review, with its quantifiers preserved

The result being reviewed is that the optimal universal exponent relating bounded-error randomized and quantum query complexity of total Boolean functions is four. OpenAI’s public description identifies the same setting: worst-case bit-query complexity, error at most \(1/3\), and unrestricted computation between queries. [GitHub](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/overview.tex?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The supplied construction gives, **for every fixed integer \(k\ge2\)** and all sufficiently large powers of four \(m\),

\[
Q(F_{k,m})
\le C_k\sqrt m\,(\log_2m)^6,
\qquad
R(F_{k,m})
\ge
\frac{1}{400}\,
\frac{m^{2-1/k}}{(\log_2m)^2}.
\]

The current source makes an important point explicit: **the same named circuit defines the function in both inequalities.** This closes an older correspondence limitation in the baseline.

The order of choices matters. Given a proposed universal exponent \(0\le\alpha<4\), first choose \(k\) so that

\[
\varepsilon=2-\frac1k-\frac{\alpha}{2}>0.
\]

Then hold \(k\) fixed and increase \(m\). The bounds imply

\[
\frac{R(F_{k,m})}{(1+Q(F_{k,m}))^\alpha}
\ge
\frac{1}{400(1+C_k)^\alpha}
\frac{m^\varepsilon}{(\log_2m)^{2+6\alpha}}
\longrightarrow\infty.
\]

Thus no universal constant can support that smaller exponent. The other direction comes from the established quartic upper bound \(D(f)=O(Q(f)^4)\), together with \(R(f)\le D(f)\). The relevant ABKRT citation is **Theorem 2, printed page 2, in arXiv:2010.12629v1**. [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12629v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Two boundaries must remain explicit:

- Each fixed \(k\) gives exponent \(4-2/k\), up to logarithmic losses. These estimates do not give an exact fourth-power separation for one fixed \(k\).
- Quantitative estimates that allow \(k\) to vary need a separate assembly argument. The current source contains useful uniform auxiliaries, but I found no exported theorem assembling the proposed growing-\(k\) total-function family.

The baseline generally gets these quantifiers right. The repairs preserve them.

## 2. The central mechanism is coherent—and worth explaining earlier

The construction uses a partial function with three distinct properties: a cheap quantum promise algorithm, strong randomized hardness, and an exact circuit recognizing its domain and value.

The exact circuit is essential. It makes proposed answers locally verifiable, which allows the eventual function to be total even when some embedded partial-function inputs violate their promises.

### Why the quantum algorithm works on all inputs

A winning column has an all-one string and a valid certificate at its true address. Its certificate points **outward** to a zero in every other column.

This direction gives the decisive asymmetry:

- A genuine winner can disqualify every opponent.
- No opponent can point to a zero in the winner’s all-one string.

The opponent’s embedded Forrelation instances need not satisfy their promises for the second assertion to hold. That is why the comparison procedure can remain useful on arbitrary inputs.

The tournament then relies on an exact conditional sampling law. If the current weights are \(w_i\), it must select a pivot with probability \(w_i/\sum_jw_j\). Complementarity of the comparisons gives

\[
\mathbb E[W_{\mathrm{new}}\mid w]
=
\frac{W^2-\sum_iw_i^2}{2W}
\le \frac W2.
\]

A dominant candidate retains substantial weight until it is selected, while the expected total weight shrinks. This yields the containment guarantee for the returned candidate list.

The baseline’s argument that amplification preserves the successful state—and therefore the required measured law—is doing real mathematical work. A high probability of returning *some* candidate would not be enough.

### Why the classical lower bound avoids a conditioning error

The classical argument couples a dummy input with a planted input. They differ only at the erased zero and the planted certificate cell, so the algorithm must query one of those locations before the two executions diverge.

The delicate point is the address simulator. Its query cap must hold **on every run**, independently of whether the original execution eventually makes the chosen column heavy.

The proof correctly uses event containment:

\[
\{\text{column is nonheavy and the target cell is queried}\}
\subseteq
\{\text{capped simulator guesses the address}\}.
\]

It therefore bounds a joint event. Conditioning the hard distribution on nonheaviness would change the experiment and would not justify the same direct-product bound.

These two transitions—exact tournament sampling and the capped simulator’s joint event—are among the strongest parts of the existing record. I recommend preserving them through any compression.

## 6. I worked through the hardest exposition change

The general Stein elimination in §3.10 is compact enough to conceal why additional terms do not appear.

The proposed replacement works through \(k=3\), with bias blocks

\[
w_s=(sX_0,\;Y_0X_1,\;Y_1).
\]

There are two eliminations.

In the first, differentiating through the first block produces a repeated cube derivative, which vanishes by multilinearity. Differentiating through the middle block survives and adds one derivative there.

In the second, the analytic weight created by the first elimination depends only on the earlier Gaussian vector \(U_0\). Differentiation in \(U_1\) leaves that weight unchanged. The repeated middle-block derivative vanishes, and the last-block contribution survives.

This exposes the two facts the induction needs:

1. A repeated derivative in the same cube coordinate vanishes.
2. Previously created weights depend only on earlier Gaussian layers.

The replacement also explains that the mean value theorem is applied once to the whole scalar function \(h_g\), producing **one common parameter** for the path sum.

A separate small example guards against an incorrect Fourier simplification. If \(b\ne c\) and

\[
g(z)=z_{0,a}z_{1,b}z_{1,c},
\]

then

\[
\mathcal E_\mu[D_{0,a}D_{1,b}g]=\mu_{1,c}.
\]

An order-two derivative at nonzero bias can therefore retain information from degree three. The example does **not** assert a nonzero final Gaussian expectation for that odd monomial.

The complete replacement is in `stein-k3-replacement.md`. It explains an existing proof; it is not presented as a newly compiled theorem.
