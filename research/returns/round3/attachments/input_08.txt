## 1. The most consequential issue: a current build does not make an old semantic review current

There are three separate questions:

1. Does a formal proposition have an accepted proof?
2. Does that proposition, including its referenced definitions, agree with the challenge?
3. Does it express the mathematics intended by the paper?

Lean’s official validation documentation explicitly separates proof validity from the meaning of the theorem statement. Reviewing the proposition and the definitions that determine its meaning remains necessary. [lean-lang.org](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The packet already handles much of the operational evidence correctly: source-bound receipts, declaration identity, legacy records, and hidden dependencies. The remaining defect concerns the **review judgment**.

For example, suppose a reviewer decides that a theorem exactly represents a paper statement. Later, someone changes a predicate used by that theorem while leaving its displayed theorem source unchanged. A new build can succeed. A regenerated challenge using the changed predicate can also agree with the solution. Neither event establishes that the original review assessed the new meaning.

The regression reproduces precisely that situation using mocked native-operation receipts. The original producer restores statement-level comparison credit. With the patch, the producer preserves the fresh build and comparison facts but marks the old review stale.

### What the patch binds

The patch adds a `review_binding` to the existing tie records. It binds the review to:

- The statement ID and exact statement text.
- The complete corrected statement row.
- The declaration identity.
- The authored judgment, including relation, proof route, premises, additional assumptions, reviewer, and notes.
- The existing complete local Lean/configuration snapshot.

The producer does **not** create or refresh these bindings automatically. Otherwise, rebuilding the corpus would effectively claim that a mathematical review had happened.

This implementation is conservative: an unrelated Lean change or a proof-only edit also invalidates the binding. That is a deliberate tradeoff. A narrower check would need reliable evidence of which elaborated definitions determine the statement’s meaning.

## 2. Equation (19) needs its equality and its bound

The complete mathematical target is

\[
\Pr\!\left[T(Y^{(0)})\text{ queries }X_I[J_I]\right]
=
\frac{\mathbb E Z}{m}
\le
\frac{4G}{m^2}.
\]

That contains two claims:

- An **equality**, connecting the independently selected column to the expected number of discovered zeros.
- An **upper bound**, controlling the discovery probability.

The paper’s authority here is §4.2, equation (19), in the [pinned OpenAI preprint](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/4eaee875/preprints/A-Nearly-Quartic-Separation-Between-Randomized-and-Quantum-Query-Complexity-October-5-2026/quartic-query-separation.pdf). I used the supplied, hash-identified paper text for the mathematics.

|---|---|
| `Paper.erasedZero_eq_expectedZeros_div` | The equality with \(\mathbb E Z/m\) |
| `Paper.expectedZeros_le_four` | \(\mathbb E Z\le 4G/m\) |
| `Paper.eq_19` | The probability upper bound only |
| `avg_foundZero_I_le` | A stronger coefficient-two bound with address data fixed |

The supplied source-current root-build receipt names these declarations. No direct comparison receipt for these individual equation-(19) declarations was supplied.

**A stronger inequality does not establish an omitted equality.** The maintained corpus already labels the old `eq-19` tie `partial`, which is appropriate. The baseline Appendix B’s older `stronger` classification needs an evidence-prose correction.

### The equality’s essential premise

Fix the complete dummy run. Suppose it discovers the designated zeros in exactly \(Z\) columns. If \(I\) is chosen uniformly and **independently of that run**, it selects one of those columns with probability \(Z/m\). Averaging gives

\[
\Pr(\text{hit the selected zero})=\mathbb E[Z/m]=\frac{\mathbb E Z}{m}.
\]

Repeated queries do not increase \(Z\): \(Z\) counts discovered columns, not query occurrences.

The isolated equality is algebraic in the address weights. Nonnegativity and normalization are needed to interpret the weighted sums as probabilities and to establish the probability bound.

### The paper’s upper-bound argument

Before a column’s zero has been discovered, condition on a positive-probability query-and-answer history **up to the current time**. Its zero remains uniform among that column’s unqueried positions. Fixing that history fixes the adaptive query schedule; the event imposes the recorded answer constraints.

Call a discovery early when fewer than \(m/2\) distinct positions in its column were queried beforehand.

- At any query time, the conditional probability of an early discovery is at most \(2/m\). Over at most \(G\) queries, the expected early count is at most \(2G/m\).
- Each remaining discovered column has received at least \(m/2\) queries. There can be at most \(2G/m\) such columns on any run.

Therefore,

\[
\mathbb E Z\le \frac{4G}{m},
\]

and the equality gives the required probability bound.

The supplied Lean development uses a different finite-tree induction that gives the stronger coefficient-two endpoint. The return explains that induction explicitly and labels it an **alternative proof route**. It does not claim that this route formalizes the printed early/late intermediate assertions.

## 3. A concrete reason independence cannot be weakened to “uniform marginal”

This was the most useful additional mathematical control.

Take four columns, each with its zero independently uniform among four rows. Query the first row in every column. Then each column’s zero is discovered with probability \(1/4\), so

\[
\mathbb E Z=1.
\]

An independent uniform \(I\) gives hit probability \(1/4\).

Now change the selection law:

- If at least one zero was discovered, choose \(I\) uniformly among the discovered columns.
- Otherwise, choose \(I\) uniformly among all four columns.

By symmetry, each column still has marginal selection probability \(1/4\). Nevertheless,

\[
\Pr(\text{hit the selected zero})
=
\Pr(Z>0)
=
1-\left(\frac34\right)^4
=
\frac{175}{256},
\]

whereas

\[
\frac{\mathbb E Z}{4}=\frac14.
\]

I checked all 256 zero-location vectors using exact rational arithmetic, including all four uniform marginals.

This is a deliberately different sampling law, not a counterexample to the paper. It demonstrates that **uniformity of \(I\)’s marginal is insufficient for the equality**. In this example \(G=m=4\), so the paper’s final upper bound is \(1\); checking that inequality alone would not expose the changed law.

The proof, script, and actual result are included under `EVID-INDEPENDENCE` and `checks/`.

## 4. The four requested semantic examples

Each example has an ordinary mathematical argument and a proposed Lean proof against the **actual project definitions**.

### One-bit identity cannot have randomized zero-query complexity

A zero-query deterministic tree returns a constant. A randomized mixture of such trees therefore has an input-independent true-output probability \(p\).

Correctness on input \(1\) requires

\[
p\ge\frac23.
\]

Correctness on input \(0\) requires

\[
1-p\ge\frac23.
\]

Together these imply \(1\ge4/3\), a contradiction.

This example detects a quantifier error: allowing a different algorithm to be chosen after seeing each input would make the zero-query claim trivially true. The proposed proof derives input-independence and normalization from the actual tree and program definitions.

### The specified one-query OR mixture attains the inclusive threshold

Choose each of these with probability \(1/3\):

- Return true.
- Query and return the first bit.
- Query and return the second bit.

Its success probabilities are:

| Input | Success |
|---|---:|
| \(00\) | \(2/3\) |
| \(01\) | \(2/3\) |
| \(10\) | \(2/3\) |
| \(11\) | \(1\) |

Every tree has depth at most one. The mixture therefore proves the one-query upper bound. Since OR takes both labels, the zero-query argument gives the matching lower bound, hence \(R(\mathrm{OR}_2)=1\).

The threshold control is deliberately precise: changing “at least \(2/3\)” to “strictly greater than \(2/3\)” makes **this mixture** fail. I do not turn that observation into an unsupported general impossibility claim about every stricter threshold.

### The true-only promise has \(R=Q=0\)

Define a partial function only on the true one-bit input, where its value is true. A constant-true algorithm is correct on every promised input and uses no queries.

The proposed classical proof provides that witness. The quantum proof uses the actual model’s constant-on-promise branch. Neither relies on an empty-infimum convention.

If the undefined input were silently changed to false, the function would become total one-bit identity and the zero-query claim would fail. This example therefore checks the promise domain as well as constant-output behavior. Its quantum scope is the zero-query branch, not all quantum oracle or measurement semantics.

### Negative Forrelation must remain outside the promise

For \(k=2\), \(t=1\), and \(m=4\), take both sign vectors to be

\[
z=(-1,1,1,1)^T.
\]

For the corresponding unnormalized Walsh matrix, \(Wz=-2z\), so

\[
z^TWz=-8.
\]

The normalization denominator is \(8\), giving \(\Phi=-1\).

The high branch requires \(\Phi\ge2^{-10}\); the low branch requires \(|\Phi|\le2^{-11}\). Both fail. This input is outside the promise. Replacing the high branch by an absolute-value condition would wrongly admit it. These conventions come from the preprint’s §2.1, equations (3)–(4).

The supplied development already contains a related Sanity witness. The new proposed test recomputes the concrete value without importing that witness; I make no novelty claim for the example.
