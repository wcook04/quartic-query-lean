## Your panel: newcomers and teachers reading the exposition

The lead paper is the **exposition**. Read every paragraph of it, work every question before reading its answer, then read the expert companion's introduction and the record passages the exposition links to.

Read as four readers, one at a time, and keep their reactions separate:

1. a research mathematician from another field (say analysis or combinatorics) who has never studied query complexity or quantum computing;
2. a strong final-year undergraduate in mathematics or computer science who has met probability, linear algebra and algorithms, but no complexity theory beyond P and NP;
3. a physicist or engineer who knows the circuit model of quantum computing from a first course, but no theoretical computer science;
4. a lecturer who would consider using the exposition in an advanced undergraduate or first-year graduate course.

Anchor what each reader knows in the supplied lecture notes and survey. Search online for how the field introduces query complexity, Forrelation and separations to newcomers: lecture notes, textbooks, talks, blog posts and popular articles.

Press especially on:

- The first page and every section opening: does each reader know why they are reading, where the argument is going and what they will be able to do at the end? Exactly where does each reader first get lost?
- Every worked example and question: what it is for, whether it is the right size, whether it is carried through to its payoff, and every place where an example is missing exactly where the reader needs one.
- Every term and symbol: where it is first used, whether it is explained in ordinary words first, and whether the reader could say what it means afterwards.
- The two hard steps, the tournament sampler and the planted lower bound: can each reader explain in their own words why they work?
- Figures: can each be read at normal size, and does it show the thing the paragraph is struggling to say?
- What the exposition overweights (arithmetic, refinements, formal-evidence bookkeeping) and underweights (intuition, motivation, pictures).
- After finishing: could each reader explain the theorem and its main idea to a friend? Write down what they would say, and compare it with what they should say. Would the lecturer assign it, and what would they need to change first?
