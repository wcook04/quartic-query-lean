# Principles from readers of AI-produced mathematics

These principles were distilled from feedback by researchers in mathematics, formal methods, interface design and science communication who have read AI-produced mathematics or interfaces built over it. They are criteria for review, not findings about these documents.

1. **Context before detail.** A human paper's introduction gives the mathematical question, the prior position, the key idea of the proof and what is new against the literature. AI-produced mathematics tends to jump into technical detail, so the reader cannot tell where the argument is going or why. A useful conceptual map is needed, not more introductory text or fewer formulas.
2. **Establish purpose and route first.** Before asking a reader to interpret technical detail, say who the document is for, why the result matters to them, what is coming and what they will be able to do. A specialist and a newcomer may need different entrances.
3. **One concrete object before the system.** Give a stranger one intelligible task and a concrete object before the larger machinery. Specific nouns and a visible operation carry more meaning than umbrella descriptions or architecture vocabulary.
4. **Name before notation; explain hypotheses.** Explain an object in ordinary mathematical language before naming it, introduce notation only when it helps, and say what each hypothesis rules out and why the conditional statement matters.
5. **Explain relationships, not only endpoints.** Showing inputs and outputs is not always enough to explain the operation connecting them. A reader should understand what a displayed relationship (a link, a graph edge, a highlighted correspondence) means without reverse-engineering the author's intention.
6. **Let evidence explain at the point of need.** Keep a short useful frame, then let the reader inspect a real example or the actual formal statement instead of repeating background.
7. **Paper statement versus formal statement.** For every result cross-checked against a formalisation, say whether the formal statement is essentially the same as the informal claim, stronger or weaker, and how. AI-written formal proofs tend to prove a nearby, easier statement. Shortening or explaining a result must not weaken a hypothesis, broaden a conclusion or turn an informal step into a formal theorem.
8. **Provenance of every relationship.** Distinguish dependencies extracted from the formal source from curated argument links and model-proposed mappings, and keep the editions of paper and formal source identifiable. Show what the authors wrote and what the proof assistant reports; a model's role is linking, and its links should say where they came from.
9. **Contribution and credit.** State what is contributed and place it against the work it uses. Separate the original theorem and proof, earlier methods, the formalisation and the explanatory work; never present a new explanation as new mathematics.
10. **Distinguish what was checked.** Separate what statement was checked, by which tool, and how another person can inspect the check. Evidence for a formal target does not by itself establish its relevance, novelty or equivalence to an informal claim.
11. **Each encounter stands alone.** A document should make sense as a first encounter without assuming knowledge of the wider programme, while letting an interested reader discover more.
12. **Judge by what a reader can do.** The test of an explanation is whether a reader can state the main idea, locate an assumption and follow a passage to the evidence needed to assess it; a good impression or working navigation is weaker evidence.
13. **Demonstrate, do not assert, an advantage.** A claimed benefit of a presentation is a hypothesis until an example shows it.
14. **Scope of explanation versus scope of dependencies.** Explaining a supporting theorem must not silently stand in for explaining the whole downstream claim.
