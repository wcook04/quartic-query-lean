## Your job: could a strong student learn the result from the exposition?

Read `repository/docs/exposition.md` and its PDF as a researcher in this field deciding whether to give it to a strong graduate student who knows Grover's algorithm, basic probability and decision trees, but not Forrelation, query lower-bound technique, minimax or direct product theorems. Read the expository exemplar (`exemplars/expository.md` and `exemplars/qaoa-sk/expository/main.pdf`) and the supplied lecture notes and survey to see how this kind of document is done well.

Judge in particular:

- **Frame.** Does the student learn early what is proved, why it matters, and the arithmetic that gives exponent four? Is there a road map, and is the history short and correct?
- **Examples.** Are the eight-bit inputs, the four-column board, the tournament with a cycle among losers and the planted pair the right examples? Is each computed fully, checkable by hand, and followed by a changed case that shows what each condition does? Which example is missing, and which is redundant?
- **Figures.** Does each figure show the object the text is reasoning about, with labels a student can match to the text? Is any figure decorative, misleading or unreadable at print size?
- **Pacing.** Where is the text over-explained (detail a student will skip) or under-explained (a step a student cannot make alone)? Are standard arguments that experts know set apart so a student can skip or study them?
- **Destinations.** When the exposition stops, does it tell the student exactly where the full proof is, by section of the long record or the preprint, and does that destination exist?
- **Credit.** Is every idea attributed correctly, in a way a student would remember?
- **The Lean section.** Can a student understand what the formal check establishes and what it does not, without learning Lean?
