## Your panel: newcomers, practitioners and teachers

The lead is the **examples-first exposition**. Use finding prefix `EX10-`. Read
it completely and attempt each question before its answer, preserving exposure
limits. Only after the first-pass freeze open the companion's introduction and
the proof passages to which the exposition sends its readers.

Use four simulated perspectives, one at a time, with distinct stated backgrounds:

1. A research mathematician from analysis or combinatorics who has not studied
   query complexity or quantum computing.
2. A strong final-year mathematics or computer-science undergraduate familiar
   with probability, linear algebra and algorithms, but little complexity theory.
3. A physicist or engineer with a first course in quantum circuits and little
   theoretical computer science.
4. A lecturer considering the paper for an advanced undergraduate or beginning
   graduate course.

After the freeze, use the supplied notes and survey to check those background
assumptions, and seek other strong introductions to query complexity, Forrelation
and separations. Lecture notes, textbooks and carefully explained talks can
illuminate a teaching choice; distinguish their evidential role from a primary
source for a theorem.

At each section opening, ask what the reader expects to understand or accomplish.
Track the first point where each perspective loses the argument, rather than
summarizing difficulty after acquiring the answer. Test whether ordinary words,
notation, pictures and examples reinforce the same model. Work an example all
the way to its payoff and ask whether it carries the essential difficulty.

The tournament and the planted lower bound should become mechanisms a reader can
reason with, not names attached to formulas. Test what the reader can predict at
each step, what a proposed alternative would do and why the actual construction
was chosen. Mathematical honesty includes clearly signalling what is proved here
and what a reader must obtain elsewhere.

Consider whether a better running example, a different instructional order,
shorter calculations or a redesigned exercise sequence could substantially
improve the whole paper. More explanation is useful only where it supplies
understanding; routine arithmetic and formal-evidence detail can obscure it.
Inspect figures at normal size, including the correspondence between their
objects and the prose. An exercise answer must explain the obstacle the attempt
revealed, not only announce the correct result.

After finishing, record a short simulated explanation of the theorem and its
main idea in each learner's own terms, and compare it with the intended content.
This is a diagnostic, not evidence of measured human learning. The lecturer
should identify the concrete changes needed for use in a course, including
prerequisites, pacing and assignments. Do not make all four readers into the same
expert or require every technical detail to be taught to every audience.
