def-partial-query

G.2. What the formal models establish

The formal model uses the domain-and-value convention of §2.1. The detailed comparison below distinguishes a partial promise from the embedding of an already total function; the construction domain wrappers prove the relevant translation and correctness conditions.

The physical compiler converts an abstract coordinate read into ordinary bit queries. An abstract read consists of a coordinate function on basis labels, an involutive permutation of those labels, and an optional sign, where the permutation preserves both its coordinate and its sign. Given a basis label, the compiler embeds the abstract register into the physical coordinate, answer-bit, and workspace registers, loads the queried bit, applies the indicated signed permutation if that bit is one, queries the unchanged coordinate again, and returns to the chosen embedding. On bit zero it is the identity and on bit one it is exactly the prescribed signed involution, so both actions preserve inner products. The finite isometries used to place the active register extend to unitaries on a sufficiently large finite workspace by completing an orthonormal basis, so the bit queries are the only input-dependent gates.

The bridges between the formal models and conventional formulations are of three kinds.

Definitions. Partial functions with a domain; one algorithm with a global cap; finite mixtures of decision trees for \(R\); programs with fixed gates and the bit oracle for \(Q\) (§§2.1–2.4).

Proved translations. Minima of nonempty sets of caps through read-all witnesses; renaming coordinates; deferred measurement as a fixed sequence of controlled phase queries with the same acceptance mass for every input and every starting vector (§2.2, §2.5).

Ordinary identifications. Finite seed spaces in place of arbitrary randomness and recursive cap compliance as observable query counts interpret the classical reduction. The basis change from a general projector to a basis-label predicate belongs to the stronger successful-state specification in G.7, which is not a premise of the headline measured-label upper bound (Appendices G.7–G.8).

The formal interpretation starts with the order of the mathematical choices: choose one algorithm, then require its correctness and its query cap for every relevant input. A theorem about a differently quantified algorithm, a different promise, or only one part of a displayed conjunction would not establish the same statement. The definitions in §§2.1–2.5 make these choices explicit. Here we describe their formal counterparts and the particular translations used later. Complete source signatures, including the circuit and workspace quantifiers, are accessible in the retained model contract and declaration correspondence; Appendix B follows every numbered source claim.

The partial function and its domain. For a finite coordinate type \(I\), a formal partial Boolean function on \(I\) is a function from bit inputs on \(I\) to optional Boolean values. A defined value records both membership in the promise and the required answer. An undefined value imposes no correctness requirement. This is the domain-and-value description of §2.1, with no assumption of totality. Totalisation embeds a total Boolean function into this type by defining its value everywhere. It is that embedding, rather than an arbitrary completion of a partial function, which appears in the total-function separation statements.

One algorithm and a global cap. The deterministic, randomized and quantum budget tests take a partial Boolean function and a natural-number budget; the complexities \(D\), \(R\) and \(Q\) are natural numbers. In the classical cases, the witness is one decision tree or one mixture of trees, with a global depth cap and correctness on all defined inputs. In the quantum case, the witness is either the explicitly constant-on-promise alternative or one normalized initial state and one capped program, chosen before the input. This includes the constant and empty-promise cases without selecting an algorithm separately for each input. None of these definitions assumes a lower-bound theorem or any property of Forrelation.

For every finite Boolean promise the read-all construction gives \[ R(f)\le D(f),\qquad Q(f)\le D(f),\qquad D(f)\le |I|. \] An equivalence \(e:I\simeq J\) simply renames the coordinates. After renaming the coordinates of \(f\) by \(e\), each of the three complexities is unchanged. These statements concern arbitrary finite promises: their only ordinary premises are the specified finite coordinate types and, for renaming, the equivalence. The classical and quantum equivalences between complexity bounds and budget tests connect the numerical complexities to the budget predicates. The uniform choice and global cap remain inside those predicates; they cannot be discarded when applying the numerical inequalities.

The physical query and its output law. With \(x\) a bit input, \(i\in I\), a response bit \(b\), and workspace label \(z\in\operatorname{Fin}(w+1)\), the oracle acts by \[ \operatorname{bitOracle}_x|i,b,z\rangle =|i,b\mathbin{\oplus}x_i,z\rangle. \] It is an involutive complex linear isometry equivalence, for every input and every basis label. The construction states these bit-oracle identities in one wrapper. For an adaptive program \(A\) and an arbitrary state vector \(\psi\), the two output masses obey \[ \operatorname{outputMass}_A(x,\psi,b)\ge0, \qquad \operatorname{outputMass}_A(x,\psi,\mathsf{true}) +\operatorname{outputMass}_A(x,\psi,\mathsf{false})=\|\psi\|^2. \] Normalization makes these masses a probability distribution. The identities themselves do not require a unit vector; this distinction matters when an intermediate branch carries unnormalized mass.

Deferring measurements with the same witnesses. For an adaptive program on \(\operatorname{Fin}(n)\) with workspace parameter \(w\) and query cap at most \(T\), the fixed-sequence normal form supplies an enlarged workspace, an entry map, a fixed sequence \(U\) and an output event \(S\). All these witnesses are chosen before the input and initial vector. For every \(x\) and \(\psi\), the mass of \(S\) after the length-\(T\) sequence on the entered vector equals the original program’s true-output mass. For a normalized vector these masses are probabilities. In symbols, the quantifier order is \[ \exists W',\operatorname{entry},U,S\quad\forall x,\psi:\qquad \operatorname{measureProb}\bigl(\operatorname{run} U(\operatorname{entry}\psi)\,x\,T,S\bigr) =\operatorname{outputMass}_A(x,\psi,\mathsf{true}). \] The full witness types are those of the linked source contract. The stated cap is the only additional premise of this translation. In particular, this output-law theorem should not be read as identifying a designated retained subsystem with an arbitrary normalized successful state; the stronger amplification issue is isolated in Appendix G.7.

The family and the universal exponent. The universal power bound is a predicate on real exponents, and the formal optimal exponent is the infimum of the exponents satisfying it. Its quantifiers range over total Boolean functions on \(\operatorname{Fin}(n)\), with \(n\ge1\) and exponent \(\alpha\ge0\), as in (1). The finite-constant and infimum formulations are explicitly related by the source wrappers. No Forrelation hypothesis belongs to this definition.

For fixed \(k\ge2\), the existential family theorem gives the real-power family bound (2), and the named-family theorem names its circuit and constants.
